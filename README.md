Part 4a: Theory Behind the Build

According to Hirschman’s theory of Exit, Voice and Loyalty (1970), when people are unhappy with an organisation, they have three possible responses. They can leave, they can speak out, or they can remain loyal and stay.
The first option is to leave. When employees leave an organisation, the company may lose valuable knowledge, skills and experience. It also loses an opportunity to understand why those employees were unhappy and what could have been done differently.
The second option is to speak out. This is important because it gives the organisation an opportunity to understand the concerns of its employees and fix things that are not working. When people speak out, the organisation can learn from their experiences and identify areas that need improvement.
The third option is loyalty, where people remain in the organisation despite being unhappy. However, just because people stay quiet does not necessarily mean that they are happy or that the organisation has a good culture. They might be afraid to speak out, or they might feel that nobody will listen to them anyway.
Now, looking at the staff app that we are developing, Niamh was saying that nobody had ever complained. But the question I have is, how can we say that nobody has complaints when we are also saying that the app should not have a complaints button?
If we do not give people a platform to raise their concerns, we are potentially encouraging them to remain silent. They might be experiencing difficulties with the app, or they might be dissatisfied with certain things, but if there is no appropriate way for them to communicate those concerns, how will we know?
This is where Hirschman’s theory becomes relevant. We cannot simply assume that the absence of complaints means that people are satisfied. Sometimes, people remain quiet because they do not feel comfortable speaking out. If we create an environment where people are expected to remain quiet and simply follow instructions without questioning anything, are we really creating a healthy organisational culture?
I understand that employees should raise their concerns in a respectful manner and should not deliberately undermine leadership. However, respecting leadership should not mean that people cannot express themselves. There should be a way for employees to communicate their concerns professionally while still respecting the people they work with.
This also brings me to Amy Edmondson’s work on psychological safety, which emphasises that people need to feel safe enough to speak up so that teams can learn.
When people feel safe enough to express themselves, they can raise concerns, ask questions, identify mistakes and suggest improvements without fearing negative consequences. This allows the team to learn from its experiences and improve the way it works.
In the context of our staff app, if we do not allow employees to voice their concerns, we are limiting our ability to learn from their experiences. The app should provide a platform where people can explain the difficulties they are experiencing, raise concerns and suggest improvements.
This does not necessarily mean that the button must be called a complaints button. It could be a feedback, review or concerns feature. What matters is that there should be a meaningful way for people to communicate what they are experiencing and for the organisation to take their feedback seriously.
I also understand that this situation might create anxiety within the team, especially when they are concerned about maintaining a good standing with stakeholders and investors. Nobody wants the organisation to appear as though it is failing or that its employees are unhappy. However, I think we also need to consider the other side of the situation.
We need to be empathetic towards the people who will be using the app and understand their experiences instead of brushing off their concerns simply because those concerns might make the organisation uncomfortable. The aim should not be to create a situation where nobody complains. The aim should be to understand why people might be dissatisfied and whether the app is actually addressing their needs.
This reminds me of Nokia and the discussions about how fear and pressure within the organisation affected the way people communicated bad news.
Research into Nokia's decline in the mobile phone market has examined how organisational pressures and fear affected the communication of information and decision-making. The broader lesson is that when people are afraid to communicate information that leadership might not want to hear, the organisation risks losing important information that could help it make better decisions.
In our case, if we decide that employees should not have a way to voice their concerns because we want to avoid complaints, we might create a similar organisational problem. We might be prioritising the comfort of the organisation over the opportunity to learn from the people who are actually using the app.
The situations are not exactly the same, but the lesson is relevant. An organisation needs to be willing to hear information that might be uncomfortable, because that information could help it identify problems and improve.
For me, this is important because the app should not simply be designed to communicate instructions from management to staff. It should also provide an appropriate way for staff members to communicate their experiences back to the organisation.
These specific theories have influenced me to make sure that we design the app in such a manner that it allows people to voice out their concerns and gives them a safe place where they can express their experiences with the app.
I believe the app should include a feedback or review feature where staff members can explain how they feel about using it, whether it is answering their problems, and whether they believe they are being treated fairly.
For example, in the case we are working on, the allocation of tips did not have sufficient transparency and predictability from the staff members' perspective. They could not clearly see how the tips were calculated, and they wanted to understand how the allocations were determined.
This shows why it is important for the app to give people a way to raise their concerns and understand the processes that affect them. If staff members do not understand how their tips are calculated, the app should help address that concern by making the calculations more transparent and providing a way for them to ask questions or give feedback.
These theories have therefore influenced my design because I believe we should not just develop an app that performs its functions but also one that listens to the people using it. We need to establish whether the product is actually addressing the problems that already exist and whether it is fair to the people using it.
The feedback feature would also help us identify problems that we might not have considered during the development process. Instead of assuming that we already know everything the users need, we would have an opportunity to learn directly from their experiences.
However, providing a feedback feature alone is not enough. The organisation must also be willing to review the feedback, identify recurring concerns and make improvements where necessary. Otherwise, we would simply be giving people a voice without doing anything about what they say.
When I look at the competitive advantage of this app, I believe that we need to consider whether it is valuable, rare, difficult to imitate and supported by the organisation.
I believe that the app becomes valuable when staff members find it useful, understand how it works and feel that their concerns are being taken seriously.
If the app allows them to understand how their tips are calculated and gives them an opportunity to raise concerns, it addresses a real problem that already exists. This can improve their experience and make them more willing to use the app.
However, I do not think that the app becomes valuable simply because it has a feedback button. Its value comes from whether it actually addresses the problems experienced by staff members.
When it comes to rarity, I do not necessarily think that this app is rare. It is an app designed to allocate tips, and other developers could create similar apps that perform the same functions.
Other competitors could also include features that allow staff members to review the app, raise concerns and understand their tip allocations. Therefore, having these features alone would not automatically make our app rare.
I think the difference would depend on how well the app understands the specific needs of its users and how effectively it responds to those needs. However, we would need to compare the app with competing products before concluding that this approach is genuinely rare.
One limitation I see when applying the VRIO framework is that I do not think this app would be particularly difficult to copy. It is not being developed for only one restaurant. It is intended to be used by several restaurants, which means that other developers could also create similar apps.
The idea of developing an app that allocates tips is not necessarily difficult to replicate. Competitors could develop similar products and even improve on the features offered by our app.
For example, another developer could create a similar app but make it more transparent and fairer to employees. If that competing app allows staff members to understand how their tips are calculated and gives them a safe place to voice their concerns, employees might prefer using that app instead.
I therefore do not think we can automatically claim that the app has a sustained competitive advantage simply because it allows people to provide feedback.
However, what could potentially make the app more competitive is the organisation's willingness to listen to users, learn from their experiences and continuously improve the product. The feature itself can be copied, but developing a culture where feedback is taken seriously and consistently used to improve the app requires an ongoing commitment.
Even then, this would not automatically make the app difficult to imitate. We would need to demonstrate that the organisation's knowledge, processes and ability to respond to users create an advantage that competitors cannot easily reproduce.
For the app to maintain any competitive advantage, the company must be willing to act on the feedback it receives.
There is no point in providing a platform where employees can raise concerns if nobody reviews those concerns or takes action. The organisation needs to have a process for listening to users, identifying recurring problems and making improvements where necessary.
This is where the theories discussed earlier become relevant to the competitive advantage of the app. If the organisation encourages people to speak up and uses their feedback to improve the product, it may develop a better understanding of what its users need.
On the other hand, if the organisation discourages people from raising concerns, it could lose the opportunity to learn and improve. Another competitor could come into the market with a product that responds more effectively to those needs.
I also have a concern about following the current client's position regarding complaints. If we develop the app according to the CEO's preference that staff members should not have a way to raise complaints, we risk creating a product that does not adequately address the concerns of the people using it.
Staff members might not understand how their tips are allocated, and they might not have an appropriate way to communicate when they feel that something is unfair.
If we follow this approach, another competitor could enter the market with a product that provides greater transparency, allows employees to voice their concerns and promotes fairer treatment. That competitor could gain an advantage because its product responds more effectively to the needs of its users.
I also think we need to consider the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 8, which promotes decent work and economic growth, and SDG 10, which focuses on reducing inequalities.
An app that affects employees' tips should consider fairness, transparency and the ability of employees to understand how their earnings are allocated. If the app does not address these concerns, it could fall short of supporting the wider goals of decent work and reducing inequalities.
If we simply follow the client's preference without considering the experiences of the staff, we might develop an app that meets management's expectations but fails to meet the needs of its users. Other apps could become more competitive by incorporating these principles into their design.
For me, this demonstrates that we should not only consider what the client wants but also the people who will be affected by the product. The app needs to consider both management's requirements and the legitimate concerns of the employees who will use it.
Promoting fairness and transparency does not mean that management loses its authority. It means that the app should be designed in a way that allows employees to understand decisions affecting them and raise concerns through an appropriate process.
Ultimately, I believe that one of the ways we can make this app competitive is by developing it in such a manner that people are given a voice rather than being silenced.
Hirschman's theory helps us understand that people may respond to dissatisfaction by leaving, speaking out or remaining loyal. Edmondson's work on psychological safety helps us understand why people need to feel safe enough to communicate their concerns so that teams can learn and improve.
These theories have influenced my decision to advocate for a feedback or review feature in the app. I believe that staff members should have an appropriate way to explain their experiences, understand how their tips are calculated and raise concerns when they feel that something is not working fairly.
At the same time, I recognise that the app is not necessarily rare or difficult to imitate. Other developers could create similar products and offer better features. Therefore, the app's competitive advantage cannot simply come from its ability to allocate tips or from having a feedback button.
Instead, I believe that its competitiveness will depend on whether it can address the problems experienced by its users better than competing products. This requires transparency, fairness, a willingness to listen and an organisational commitment to act on feedback.
The app should not only solve the problems identified by management; it should also provide a way for the people using it to show us whether those problems have actually been solved.
If we want the app to remain competitive, we need to design it in a way that allows employees to be heard, enables the organisation to learn from their experiences, and supports fairer and more transparent tip allocation. This would improve the user experience while helping the product align with the principles behind SDG 8 and SDG 10.
For me, the key point is that a competitive product should not only serve the interests of the business. It should also respond to the needs of the people using it. When employees feel that the app is useful, transparent and responsive to their concerns, they have a stronger reason to trust it and use it. That is the kind of product I believe we should aim to develop



Part 4b: Decision Record
The client and the CEO wanted the staff app to have no complaints button, and Niamh reportedly said nobody had complained. I considered removing the feature or keeping a way for staff to raise concerns. I chose to retain a concern feature because staff needed to understand how tips were calculated and have a way to raise questions. My criteria were fairness, transparency, privacy, employee voice and psychological safety. And also to reflect contribution to the SDG 6 and 8. I would reconsider the design if there were evidence that an existing, accessible and safe channel allowed staff to raise concerns and receive responses. The underlying need may have been to avoid conflict and protect relationships. My build addresses this by providing a private, local record of concerns, although it does not send them to management. The feature must be understood as a simulated feedback mechanism, not a complete complaints process.

While reviewing the staff concerns feature on my website, I realised that my original wording was misleading. It said that concerns would not be sent to Sciar, even though Sciar is the company developing the product. If the company cannot receive or review staff feedback, how will it understand users’ concerns and improve the product?

I realised that protecting staff privacy should not mean preventing the company from learning from their experiences. A better approach would be to explore an anonymous reporting feature that allows staff to raise concerns without automatically attaching their names or personal identifiers. Sciar could then review the feedback, identify recurring issues and improve the product, while limiting access to identifying information.

However, anonymity would need to be designed carefully. The system should explain what information is collected, who can access it and whether a concern can be traced back to the person who submitted it. I therefore want to correct the wording on my website so that it accurately describes how the current prototype works and explore a more appropriate approach to privacy and feedback handling.
